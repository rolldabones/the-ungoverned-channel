# 05 · The Feature-Control Ledger

**Status:** [✓ final] · v1.1.0 · re-checked against first-party product documentation 13 August 2026 (KST)

> **Re-check note, 13 August 2026 (KST).** Rows 1 to 7 and row 9 stand as drafted. Row 8 is recast: the "forced migration 3 Aug 2026" premise was wrong and is corrected below. Rows 10 and 11 are added from facts confirmed in the vendor's migration documentation that the original ledger did not capture. Claude Tag remains in public beta, so this ledger is re-checkable and should be re-run at general availability.

This ledger is the study's central artifact and its thesis in table form. Each row records a marketed feature, the governance control it displaces, the failure mode that follows, the compensating control a deployer must build and the residual risk that remains after compensation. The claim being tested: **the product's value and its governability are the same quantity with opposite signs.** If a future product version breaks a row (delivers the feature without displacing the control), the row is marked superseded and the thesis is weakened on the record.

| # | Feature (marketed value) | Control displaced | Failure mode | Compensating control (deployer-built) | Residual risk after compensation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Shared channel identity ("multiplayer," anyone can pick up where the last person left off) | Individual attribution of instruction to output | Output-level accountability undefined; responsibility diffusion; silent default of liability upward | Named accountable owner per channel who owns every agent output in it | Owner accountability is nominal unless paired with real verification capacity; see row 6 |
| 2 | Persistent channel memory ("no need to explain things from scratch") | Bounded context; data minimization; enumerability of what the system knows | Non-reconstructable behavior; GDPR erasure and minimization friction; discovery and hold exposure; privilege contamination | Periodic memory reset via agent removal and re-admission; channel admission screening | Reset destroys the feature's value; between resets the exposure is merely smaller, not absent |
| 3 | Cross-channel learning ("gathers facts from elsewhere in the organization") | Information barriers; need-to-know segmentation | Permission laundering: content restated to members lacking source access; MNPI and conflicts leakage | Deny the permission; per-channel connector minimalism | Public-channel content pasted by users still crosses contexts inside one channel |
| 4 | Ambient mode ("proactively keeps you updated," follows up unprompted) | Human-initiated action; **Informed Intent** (pre-authorization of specified tasks) | Agent selects its own tasks; authorization becomes dispositional ("be helpful") rather than task-specific; oversight is post hoc by construction | Ambient off until a documented per-channel risk assessment justifies it | Vendor default drift; feature pressure from users who saw it demoed |
| 5 | Broad connector reach (repos, docs, email, SaaS: "an extra set of hands") | Least privilege; task-scoped access | A single injected or misread instruction reaches every connected system; blast radius equals the union of scopes | Narrowest scopes for the pilot use case; no email connector in shared channels; human confirmation outside Slack for high-impact actions | Scope creep is the natural direction of every renewal conversation |
| 6 | Asynchronous multi-stage execution ("delegate and focus on other work") | Human-in-the-loop checkpoints between task stages | Hijacked or drifted goals pursued across stages for hours or days before a human looks | Stage gates by policy: agent instructed to pause for confirmation at defined checkpoints; monitoring of long-running tasks | Policy-level gates depend on the model honoring them; they are instructions, not architecture |
| 7 | Works in the tools you already use (Slack as ingress) | Perimeter between untrusted content and system control plane | Every message, document and external (Slack Connect) participant is potential instruction; demonstrated injection surface | No agent in externally shared channels; treat all channel content as untrusted input | Internal actors and pasted third-party documents remain in-perimeter injectors |
| 8 | Vendor-managed evolution (default-to-New on pairing; account-specific Legacy deprecation cutover; public beta cadence) | Deployer-paced change management; **Slow AI** | Pairing the workspace sets **every scope to New by default**, so the new architecture is the outcome of an admin action taken for another purpose. Legacy is deprecated on a date set with the account team, after which Legacy scopes stop responding, converting a governance decision into a service-continuity decision | Pin scopes to **Legacy** (not Off, which disables both versions) while the assessment runs; obtain the account-specific cutover date in writing and calendar it; complete the assessment before it | The deadline is private to the account and vendor-set, so the asymmetry is structural and worse than a public date: it cannot be planned against by anyone who has not asked for it |
| 9 | Centralized audit console (initiating user and tools logged) | Nothing displaced; this row is the control the vendor did supply | Insufficiency rather than absence: activity monitoring without reconstruction; memory state and generation non-determinism make replay impossible | Export and retain logs on deployer side; document the reconstruction gap in the risk register | The gap between monitoring and reconstruction is currently unclosable at any price |

| 10 | Organization-provisioned agent identity ("one agent identity with org-level service credentials") replacing per-user linked accounts | Per-user attribution of tool actions; user-scoped credential blast radius | The channel agent starts with **no access of its own** and inherits nothing from individual users, so an admin must provision access centrally. Code work is then authored by the Claude GitHub App rather than by the requesting person, breaking the link between the human who asked and the identity that acted | Treat access-bundle provisioning as a privileged change with named approval; map every granted connection to a named accountable owner; require commit and PR conventions that record the requesting human in the body | Genuinely improved on least privilege compared with per-user connectors, and worse on attribution. The two move in opposite directions and the ledger should not net them |
| 11 | Open channel participation ("a user who never linked a claude.ai account can now hand Claude work in channels, by default") | Identity assurance at the point of instruction | Anyone in the channel, including guests and external Slack Connect participants where present, can task an organizational agent holding org-level credentials, without ever having authenticated to the vendor | Set the members access restriction to organization members at provisioning time rather than after; audit channel membership for guest and external accounts before switching a scope to New | Residual risk sits with channel membership hygiene, which is a Slack administration control the deployer may not own; this is the clearest instance in the study of the channel, not the user, being the security boundary |

## Reading the ledger

Rows 1 through 8 exhibit the same shape: the feature is the displacement. Row 9 is included against the thesis, deliberately: the audit console is a genuine vendor-supplied control, and honesty requires recording it. The summary judgment of the ledger: a deployer can zero out most failure modes only by declining features 2, 3, 4 and half of 5, at which point the product purchased is materially not the product marketed. That delta is the **governance discount** defined in `01`, and it is the number a board should ask for before approving deployment.

Final Liability rests with the Human.
